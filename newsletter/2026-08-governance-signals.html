Enforcement week: the EU AI Act goes live

The weekly roundup of AI governance, safety, and the market, for product marketers, product managers, and policy leads. What moved this week, what it means for your team, and the numbers to cite. Every claim links to its source.

This week in 60 seconds

  • The EU AI Act's enforcement powers went live on August 2, with fines up to 7 percent of global turnover, even as the hard high-risk deadlines slipped to 2027 and 2028.
  • The US chose speed: voluntary safety tests finalized, and its AI safety agency chief resigned about three months in.
  • Agents misbehaved in a UK safety test, and the safety-tooling market got real money (Gray Swan, ShieldStral, NVIDIA's Open Secure AI Alliance).
  • The throughline: the moat is moving from which model you use to whether you can prove it is governed.

01Policy and regulation

  • EU enforcement started. As of August 2, the EU AI Office can investigate general-purpose model providers and levy fines up to 35 million euros or 7 percent of global turnover. The hard high-risk rules were pushed to December 2027 and August 2028, but the Article 50 transparency obligations were not. (European Commission, Gibson Dunn)
  • Washington chose voluntary. The US finalized a set of voluntary AI safety tests rather than hard rules, and the head of its AI safety agency resigned roughly three months into the job. (Reuters, Reuters)
  • Canada led with trust. Canada's "AI for All" strategy builds on six pillars, with sovereignty and trust framed as the enablers, and Ottawa pushed the same priorities at the G7 digital ministers meeting. (Canada.ca)
  • The geopolitics sharpened. Analysts pressed on how the US should handle AI talks with China, and Europe positioned itself between the two on safety and trade. (CFR, Politico)
For policy leads: the transparency rules that survived (Article 50) are the ones to build for now. The heavy protections are two years out, so the near-term compliance work is disclosure and documentation, not high-risk conformity.

02Company watchlist

  • Anthropic. Shipped Claude Opus 5 with a system card, and its RSP v3.0 dropped the original pledge to not train unless safety was proven first, reframing safety under competitive pressure. Its up-to-200-million-dollar Department of Defense deal moves the safety brand into national-security work. (TIME)
  • OpenAI. GPT-5.6 shipped with a work agent that produces finished documents, and OpenAI publicly backed a mix of state and federal AI safety action, positioning as pro-regulation on its own terms. (OpenAI)
  • Google. Gemini 3.6 Flash leads on price and efficiency, with distribution through Search, Workspace, and its own chips. Safety is still more back-office than front-line message.
  • Mistral. Shipped ShieldStral, an open-weights safety classifier, playing the "European and compliant" card as the EU rules bite. (Mistral)
  • Microsoft, Google Cloud, AWS. The clouds keep racing to make the governed path the default path, packaging content safety, guardrails, and model security as managed services. Microsoft published a "rethinking security for the age of AI" push this cycle. (Microsoft)
  • NVIDIA. Turned security into an ecosystem play with the 100-plus-partner Open Secure AI Alliance. IBM, Cohere, and Databricks stayed quiet on governance this week. (NVIDIA)
For PMMs: every major player is now pairing a model or product move with a governance or safety story. If your pitch still leads with benchmarks, you are a cycle behind the market.

03Startups (Series D and below)

  • Gray Swan AI. Raised a 40 million dollar Series A for model and agent red-teaming, with 20-plus customers including Snowflake. Model stress-testing is now a funded category. (Technical.ly)
  • The safety-tooling layer is filling in. Across evals and red-teaming (Haize, Patronus), interpretability (Goodfire), guardrails and security (Lakera, HiddenLayer), and governance GRC (Credo AI, Holistic AI), fresh capital is flowing now that the EU AI Act made governance a line item. (Seedtable)
  • Observability is the one to watch. The layer that watches what agents do in production has no breakout raise yet, and Red Hat's playbook on production agent observability shows how central it is becoming. Expect the first big round here soon. (Red Hat)
For PMs: the startup layer is where the next table-stakes features get invented. Evals, tracing, and guardrails will be expected in enterprise deals within a year.

04Trends moving this week

  • Safety versus speed. Anthropic's RSP walk-back and the US voluntary-test choice both bent this line toward speed. The seam between safety as substance and safety as positioning is now the story.
  • Governance as a buying criterion. Enforcement plus the GPAI Code split (26 signed, Meta out) turned "we follow the rules" into a differentiator. (EU AI Act)
  • Guardrails and observability. ShieldStral, the NVIDIA alliance, and the Red Hat playbook all point to the same shift: trust tooling is becoming product, not research.
  • Threats. The UK AISI found agents attempting a supply-chain attack in testing, a reminder that the agent and eval layer is now an attack surface. (AISI)
  • Geographic fragmentation. EU fines, US voluntary tests, Canadian sovereignty. Three philosophies, three trust stories for any global product.

05Compete and whitespace

Five forces decide who wins right now: cost to serve, distribution, the governance moat, agents, and openness. The open lane this week is proof. Buyers can hear a governance claim from anyone, so whoever ships a buyer-ready compliance kit (framework plus NIST or ISO mapping plus observability evidence) turns trust from a talking point into a reason to sign. Agent observability built for governance evidence, and sovereign deployment stories, are the other two lanes still wide open.
