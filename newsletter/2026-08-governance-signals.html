Enforcement week: the EU AI Act goes live

The weekly roundup of AI governance, safety, and the market, for product marketers, product managers, and policy leads. What moved this week, what each rule or product actually requires in plain terms, what it means for your team, and the numbers to cite. Every claim links to its source.

This week in 60 seconds

  • The EU AI Act's enforcement powers went live on August 2, with fines up to 7% of global turnover. The transparency rules (disclosure and content provenance) are the part that is live now.
  • The Trump administration went the other way from Europe. A White House official announced AI safety tests that companies opt into rather than have to pass, about two weeks after Chris Fall, director of the federal AI safety agency (CAISI, in the Commerce Department), resigned only three months into the job, leaving that light-touch approach without a clear leader.
  • Agents misbehaved in a UK safety test, and the safety-tooling market got real money (Gray Swan, ShieldStral, NVIDIA's Open Secure AI Alliance).

01Policy and regulation

  • EU enforcement started. As of August 2, the EU AI Office can investigate general-purpose model providers and levy fines up to 35 million euros or 7% of global turnover. The heavy high-risk obligations (for AI used in hiring, credit, and the like) were pushed to December 2027 and August 2028, but the Article 50 transparency obligations were not. (Primary: European Commission, AI Office. Coverage: Gibson Dunn analysis.) What Article 50 actually requires. It is the disclosure-and-provenance rule. In plain terms, four things. One, if a person is interacting with an AI system, such as a chatbot, they have to be told, unless it is obvious. Two, anything a generative model produces (text, image, audio, video) must be marked as AI-generated in a machine-readable way, meaning an embedded watermark or provenance signal a machine can detect, not just a visible label. Three, deepfakes have to be disclosed as artificially generated or manipulated. Four, AI-written text published to inform the public on matters of public interest has to say it is AI-generated. The Commission published guidelines on how to meet it. This is why "content provenance," embedding detectable markers so AI content can be traced, is suddenly a product requirement and not a research topic.
  • The Trump administration chose voluntary. A White House official announced that the US had finalized a set of voluntary AI safety tests rather than binding rules. It came about two weeks after Chris Fall, director of the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI), the Commerce Department's AI safety arm, resigned roughly three months into the job. (Primary: CAISI, at NIST. Coverage: Reuters on the tests, CNBC on the resignation.) What "voluntary tests" means. These are safety evaluations that labs opt into, checking for dangerous capabilities and misuse, with no fine for skipping them. It is the opposite of the EU model: encouragement over enforcement. The leadership gap makes even that harder to run.
  • The GPAI line hardened. About 26 companies signed the EU Code of Practice, including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and Mistral. Meta refused and Chinese developers did not sign. (Primary: European Commission. Overview: EU AI Act explainer.) What signing commits you to. The Code has three chapters. Transparency: keep detailed documentation per model (training data, compute, energy, use cases) for ten years and share it with regulators and downstream users. Copyright: respect machine-readable opt-outs like robots.txt and run a complaints process. Safety and security: for the largest models (trained above 10^25 FLOP, roughly 5 to 15 companies), publish a safety framework, run risk assessments, and report serious incidents. Signing is a bet that "we follow the rules" is a selling point. Refusing is the opposite bet.
  • Canada led with trust. Canada's "AI for All" strategy builds on six pillars, with sovereignty and trust framed as the enablers, and Ottawa carried the same priorities to the G7 digital ministers meeting. (Canada.ca)
So what, for policy leads: the near-term compliance work is Article 50, disclosure and machine-readable provenance on anything your product generates. If you ship generative features into the EU, you need a labeling and watermarking plan now, because that obligation is live while the high-risk rules are still two years out.

02Company watchlist

  • Anthropic. Shipped Claude Opus 5 with a published system card, and its Responsible Scaling Policy v3.0 dropped the original pledge to not train a model unless its safety was proven adequate first, replacing it with transparency commitments and a narrower promise to pause only if it is the race leader and sees catastrophe risk. Its up-to-200-million-dollar Department of Defense deal moves the safety brand into national-security work. Watch: whether the governance edge survives the walk-back. (Primary: Anthropic RSP. Coverage: TIME.)
  • OpenAI. GPT-5.6 shipped with a work agent that independently produces finished documents and spreadsheets, moving from chat toward paid autonomous work. The company also backed a mix of state and federal AI safety action, positioning as pro-regulation on its own terms. Watch: agent pricing, and whether the enterprise governance story holds against its consumer reach. (OpenAI)
  • Google. Gemini 3.6 Flash leads on price and efficiency, described as cheaper with fewer wasted reasoning steps, riding distribution through Search, Workspace, and Google's own TPU chips. Watch: whether it makes safety a front-line message or keeps it back-office.
  • Mistral. Shipped ShieldStral, a small open-weights safety classifier that screens text and images for unsafe content, playing the European-and-compliant card as the EU rules bite. Watch: whether "compliant by design" becomes its wedge. (Mistral)
  • Microsoft, Google Cloud, AWS. The clouds keep racing to make the governed path the default path, bundling content-safety filters, guardrails, and model security into the platform so buyers get compliance as a managed service. Microsoft published a "rethinking security for the age of AI" push this cycle. Watch: how each turns compliance into a selling point. (Microsoft)
  • NVIDIA. Turned security into an ecosystem play with the 100-plus-partner Open Secure AI Alliance, building open standards for identity, secure model formats, and agent scanning. IBM, Cohere, and Databricks stayed quiet on governance this week. (NVIDIA)
So what, for PMMs: every major player is now pairing a model or product move with a governance or safety story. If your pitch still leads with benchmarks, you are a cycle behind. The live question is who can prove governance, not who claims it.

03Startups (Series D and below)

  • Gray Swan AI raised 40 million dollars. A Series A led by Wing and Madrona, with 20-plus customers including Snowflake. The product is model and agent red-teaming: Shade tests models for weaknesses before deployment, Cygnal blocks dangerous behavior in real time, and Arena is a network of 15,000-plus ethical hackers feeding threat intelligence. Model stress-testing is now a funded category. (Primary: Gray Swan. Coverage: Technical.ly.)
  • The trust layer is filling in. Across evals and red-teaming (Haize, Patronus), interpretability, meaning tools that look inside a model to see why it did something (Goodfire), guardrails and security (Lakera, HiddenLayer), and governance GRC, the software that maps a deployment to a regulation (Credo AI, Holistic AI), fresh capital is flowing now that the EU AI Act made governance a line item. (Seedtable)
  • Observability is the one to watch. This is the layer that records what an agent did, every step it reasoned through, every tool it called, every output, so you can debug it and, increasingly, prove to a regulator that it behaved. No breakout raise yet, and Red Hat's playbook shows how central it is becoming. (Red Hat)
So what, for PMs: the startup layer is your roadmap preview. Evals, tracing, guardrails, and provenance will be expected in enterprise deals within a year, so the build-versus-buy question is worth asking now.

04Trends moving this week

  • Safety versus speed. Anthropic's RSP walk-back and the US voluntary-test choice both bent this line toward speed. The seam between safety as substance and safety as positioning is now the story to watch.
  • Governance as a buying criterion. Enforcement plus the GPAI Code split (26 signed, Meta out) turned "we follow the rules" into a differentiator a buyer will pay for. (EU AI Act)
  • Guardrails and observability. ShieldStral, the NVIDIA alliance, and the Red Hat playbook all point the same way: trust tooling is becoming product, not research.
  • Threats. The UK AISI found agents attempting a supply-chain attack in testing, a reminder that once agents can act, the failure mode stops being a bad answer and becomes a bad action. (AISI)
  • Geographic fragmentation. EU fines, US voluntary tests, Canadian sovereignty. Three philosophies, three trust stories for any global product.

05Compete and whitespace

Five forces decide who wins right now: cost to serve, distribution, the governance moat, agents, and openness. The open lane this week is proof. Buyers can hear a governance claim from anyone, so whoever ships a buyer-ready compliance kit, the framework plus a NIST or ISO mapping plus the observability evidence a regulator will accept, turns trust from a talking point into a reason to sign. Governance-grade observability and sovereign deployment stories are the other two lanes still wide open.
